01 / MAGNITUDE

Build a triangle from velocities.

Take two nearby times separated by Δt. The object moves through an angle Δθ on a circle of radius r, at constant speed v. Its two velocity vectors have equal length v and the same angle Δθ between them, because both are perpendicular to their respective radii.

v Δt = r Δθ   ⇒   Δθ/Δt = v/r

Move the two velocity vectors so their tails coincide. Translating a vector does not change it. The arrow from the tip of v₁ to the tip of v₂ is Δv = v₂ − v₁. These three sides form an isosceles triangle.

Why is |Δv| = 2v sin(Δθ/2)?

Bisect the triangle at its shared tail. Each half is a right triangle with hypotenuse v, angle Δθ/2 and opposite side |Δv|/2. So:

sin(Δθ/2) = (|Δv|/2)/v
⇒   |Δv| = 2v sin(Δθ/2)

Acceleration is the limiting rate of change of velocity:

a = limΔt→0 |Δv|/Δt
= v limΔt→0 [sin(Δθ/2)Δθ/2 · ΔθΔt]
= v · 1 · (v/r) = v²/r

Angles in these equations are in radians. We use limx→0 sin(x)/x = 1. The slider displays degrees for convenience.

02 / DIRECTION

Subtract the tangents. Find the inward arrow.

Choose two positions equally spaced before and after the rightmost point of the circle, moving counterclockwise. Let their angles be −δ and +δ, where δ = Δθ/2.

v₁ = v(sin δ, cos δ)
v₂ = v(−sin δ, cos δ)
Δv = v₂ − v₁ = (−2v sin δ, 0)

The vertical components cancel exactly. The horizontal component is negative, so Δv points left—directly inward from the midpoint of the arc. Dividing by positive Δt preserves this direction.

As Δt → 0, the two positions converge to that midpoint. Thus the instantaneous acceleration there points toward the centre. Rotating the whole construction proves the same result at every point on the circle.

For a finite interval, Δv points inward relative to the arc midpoint, not exactly along the inward radius at either endpoint. The animation uses symmetric endpoints to show this distinction. Reversing the motion gives the same inward direction.

03 / VECTOR PROOF

Two quarter turns make the direction exact.

Let r be the position vector measured from the centre, with |r| = r. Define R as a counterclockwise 90° rotation. For counterclockwise uniform circular motion, the tangent velocity is:

v = (v/r) R(r)

Why? R turns the outward radius onto the tangent and preserves its length r. Multiplying by v/r changes that length to v. At the two times:

v₁ = (v/r) R(r₁),   v₂ = (v/r) R(r₂)
Δv = (v/r)[R(r₂) − R(r₁)]
= (v/r) R(Δr)

Rotation is linear and continuous, so it can pass through the limit. Since v and r are constant:

a = limΔt→0 Δv/Δt
= (v/r) R(limΔt→0 Δr/Δt)
= (v/r) R(v)
= (v²/r²) R(R(r))
= −(v²/r²)r

Two 90° rotations make a 180° rotation: R(R(r)) = −r. The minus sign proves that acceleration points opposite the outward radius, directly toward the centre.

What changes for clockwise motion?

Use S, a clockwise 90° rotation: S(x,y) = (y,−x). Then v = (v/r)S(r) and S(S(r)) = −r. The same proof gives the same inward acceleration.

04 / THE ALGEBRA

Why can R move outside the subtraction?

A counterclockwise quarter turn maps R(x,y) = (−y,x). Write r₁ = (x₁,y₁) and r₂ = (x₂,y₂). Then:

R(r₂) − R(r₁)
= (−y₂,x₂) − (−y₁,x₁)
= (−(y₂−y₁), x₂−x₁)
= R(r₂ − r₁) = R(Δr)

Therefore, the step used above follows directly:

Δv = (v/r) R(Δr)

The same coordinate rule shows that R(cu) = cR(u) and that coordinate limits commute with R. These facts justify the division and limit in the vector proof.

The complete result

a = −(v²/r²)r = −(v²/r) r̂

Magnitude: v²/r. Direction: inward. Constant speed still produces acceleration because velocity changes direction.
