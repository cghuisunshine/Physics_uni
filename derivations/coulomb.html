Chapter 21 · Coulomb’s Law

Coulomb’s law and superposition

Coulomb’s inverse-square law is an empirical electrostatic law, not an algebraic theorem. Its vector form encodes attraction or repulsion, and superposition builds the force from several charges.

Fundamental law / postulate𝐅₁←₂ = ke q₁q₂(𝐫₁ − 𝐫₂)/|𝐫₁ − 𝐫₂|³Why this is a starting point ↓

What the symbols mean

q₁, q₂: signed charges (C); 𝐫₁, 𝐫₂: their positions; ke = 1/(4πε₀); 𝐅₁←₂: force on charge 1 from charge 2.

When this applies

Stationary point charges in vacuum, or spherical distributions used only where the point-charge equivalence applies.

01

State the measured magnitude law

The magnitude is proportional to the product of charge magnitudes and inversely proportional to their separation squared.

Fundamental law / postulateF = ke|q₁q₂|/r²Why this is a starting point ↓
02

Supply a direction

The unit vector from charge 2 toward charge 1 is (𝐫₁ − 𝐫₂)/r. Multiplying it by keq₁q₂/r² gives repulsion for equal signs and attraction for opposite signs.

Derived resultr = |𝐫₁ − 𝐫₂|See the derivation ↓

Add independent contributions

Electrostatic superposition states that the total force is the vector sum of the pairwise forces. Resolve into components when the charges are not collinear.

Check the result

A worked example

Two charges +1 μC and −2 μC separated by 0.10 m attract with force magnitude about 1.80 N using ke ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².

Derivation behind this calculation ↗

A point to keep straight

Do not add force magnitudes before accounting for direction. A scalar product of signed charges in the magnitude formula requires an absolute value.
